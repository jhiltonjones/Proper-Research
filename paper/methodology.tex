\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section describes the magnetic beam-steering system used throughout
the paper: the Cosserat-rod beam model and its magnetic actuation law
(Sec.~\ref{sec:beam-model}), the control (output) Jacobian obtained from
it (Sec.~\ref{sec:jacobian}), the offline reference-trajectory generator
(Sec.~\ref{sec:planner}), the two closed-loop controllers compared in this
work (Sec.~\ref{sec:control}), the real-time system that executes them on
hardware (Sec.~\ref{sec:online}), and the experimental hardware/timing
parameters used to generate the results reported elsewhere in the paper
(Sec.~\ref{sec:expsetup}).

\subsection{System Overview}
\label{sec:overview}

The system steers a slender, magnetically-responsive beam (guidewire) that
is fed axially through a fixed insertion point by a linear advancer, while
a permanent magnet mounted on the end-effector of a 6-DoF robot arm
supplies the external field that bends the beam. A camera observes the
beam and reports the tip position (and, where available, tip tangent
direction) to the controller at each control step.

The physical control input to the beam is the pose of the actuating
magnet together with the instantaneous insertion length,
$\theta = [\,p_a;\,\phi_a;\,L\,]\in\mathbb{R}^{7}$, where $p_a\in\mathbb{R}^3$
is the magnet position, $\phi_a\in\mathbb{R}^3$ is a minimal (e.g.\
axis--angle) parameterization of its orientation, and $L$ is the inserted
beam length. Because the magnet is rigidly mounted on the robot
end-effector, $\theta$'s pose components are themselves a function of the
robot's six joint angles $q\in\mathbb{R}^6$ through the arm's own forward
kinematics. The quantity actually commanded and measured at each control
step is therefore the joint-and-insertion configuration
\begin{equation}
z = [\,q;\,L\,] \in \mathbb{R}^{7},
\label{eq:config}
\end{equation}
and its time-derivative $v=\dot z$ (six joint velocities and one insertion
rate) is the control signal both controllers below compute. Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}
summarizes the real-time data flow between vision, the controller, and the
robot/advancer actuators; Sec.~\ref{sec:online} describes it in detail.

\subsection{Magnetic Beam Model}
\label{sec:beam-model}

The beam is modeled as a Cosserat rod of length $L$ and circular cross
section, following standard geometrically-exact rod
theory~\cite{rucker2010geometrically}, represented in
piecewise-constant-strain (PCS) form: the centerline is divided into $n_s$
nodes ($n_s-1$ constant-strain segments), and the local strain
$u(s)=[u_1(s);u_2(s);u_3(s)]$ (torsion and the two principal bending
curvatures, in the body frame) is constant within each segment. The
material is linear elastic with cross-sectional stiffness
\begin{equation}
K = \operatorname{diag}(GJ,\,EI,\,EI),
\label{eq:stiffness}
\end{equation}
where $E$ and $G$ are the (composite) Young's and shear moduli, and $I$,
$J$ are the second moment of area and polar moment of area of the circular
cross section. $E$ is obtained by fitting the model to measured beam
deflections under known actuation (Sec.~\ref{sec:online} and the
experimental section of the paper); $G$ follows from $E$ and an assumed
Poisson's ratio $\nu$ via $G=E/(2(1+\nu))$.

\subsubsection{Magnetic actuation}
The actuating magnet is modeled as an ideal point dipole of moment $m_a$
located at $p_a$. At a point $r$ along the beam it produces the field
\begin{equation}
B(r;\theta) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi\lVert R\rVert^3}\Big(3(\hat R\cdot m_a)\hat R - m_a\Big),
\quad R = r-p_a,\ \hat R=\tfrac{R}{\lVert R\rVert},
\label{eq:dipole-field}
\end{equation}
the standard point-dipole field used throughout the magnetic-continuum-robot
literature \cite{fu2023magnetic,kratchman2017guiding}. The beam carries a
local magnetic moment $m(s)$ (a fixed, hard-magnetized profile, or a
field-dependent/induced one, depending on the beam's fabrication), and the
magnetic potential energy is
\begin{equation}
W_m[u;\theta] = -\int_0^L m(s)\cdot B\big(r(s;u);\theta\big)\,ds,
\label{eq:magnetic-energy}
\end{equation}
evaluated at each segment midpoint under the PCS discretization.

\subsubsection{Equilibrium}
The beam's elastic (bending/twist) energy is
\begin{equation}
W_{el}[u] = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^L \big(u(s)-u^\circ\big)^{\!\top} K \big(u(s)-u^\circ\big)\,ds,
\label{eq:elastic-energy}
\end{equation}
with $u^\circ$ the (zero, for an initially straight beam) rest strain.
Adding optional gravitational and lumen-contact penalty terms
$W_g[u]$, $W_{cf}[u]$ where relevant, the total potential energy is
\begin{equation}
\Pi(u;\theta) = W_{el}[u] + W_m[u;\theta] + W_g[u] + W_{cf}[u],
\label{eq:total-energy}
\end{equation}
and the beam's quasi-static shape at a given control input is the strain
field that minimizes it,
\begin{equation}
u^*(\theta) = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_u \Pi(u;\theta).
\label{eq:equilibrium}
\end{equation}
\eqref{eq:equilibrium} is solved with a quasi-Newton, bound-constrained
(L-BFGS-B~\cite{byrd1995lbfgsb}) minimizer, using analytic energy
gradients. Because the physical process is one of
continuous beam insertion, the solve is performed with quasi-static
continuation in $L$: the equilibrium at one insertion length is used to
warm-start the solve at the next, which both accelerates convergence and
avoids convergence to non-physical local equilibria that an unwarmed solve
can find in this non-convex energy landscape.

Given the equilibrium strain field $u^*(\theta)$, the centerline pose
$(p(s),R(s))$ is recovered by the standard Cosserat-rod reconstruction
\begin{equation}
p'(s) = R(s)e_3, \qquad R'(s) = R(s)\,\widehat{u(s)},
\label{eq:reconstruction}
\end{equation}
($\widehat{(\cdot)}$ the skew-symmetric matrix operator) integrated along
the beam from the (fixed) insertion point. The quantity ultimately
regulated by the controllers below is the beam-tip pose,
\begin{equation}
y(\theta) = \big[\,p(L);\,R(L)e_3\,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^6,
\label{eq:output}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ tip position and tip tangent direction, though the experiments in
this paper track tip \emph{position} only ($y=p(L)\in\mathbb{R}^3$); the
tangent rows of the Jacobian derived next are retained for completeness
and future use.

\subsection{Control (Output) Jacobian}
\label{sec:jacobian}

Both the offline planner (Sec.~\ref{sec:planner}) and the closed-loop
controllers (Sec.~\ref{sec:control}) require the sensitivity of the tip
output \eqref{eq:output} to the control input, $J_b(\theta)=\partial
y/\partial\theta$. Because $y$ depends on $\theta$ only through the
equilibrium strain field $u^*(\theta)$ (and, directly, through the
insertion length component of $\theta$), $J_b$ is obtained via the
implicit function theorem rather than by repeated re-solves of
\eqref{eq:equilibrium}. Differentiating the first-order optimality
condition $\nabla_u\Pi(u^*;\theta)=0$ with respect to $\theta$ gives
\begin{equation}
H\,\frac{\partial u^*}{\partial\theta} + G = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\partial u^*}{\partial\theta} = -H^{-1}G,
\label{eq:ift}
\end{equation}
where $H=\nabla^2_{uu}\Pi$ is the Hessian of the total energy at the
equilibrium and $G=\nabla^2_{u\theta}\Pi$ is the mixed second derivative,
both obtained by finite differences of the analytically-available energy
gradient about the converged equilibrium. The beam Jacobian then follows
by the chain rule,
\begin{equation}
J_b(\theta) = \frac{\partial y}{\partial u}\frac{\partial u^*}{\partial\theta}
+ \left.\frac{\partial y}{\partial\theta}\right\rvert_{u=u^*}
\ \in\ \mathbb{R}^{6\times7},
\label{eq:jacobian-chain}
\end{equation}
where the second (direct) term captures $y$'s explicit dependence on
$\theta$ at fixed strain (e.g.\ through the insertion length). This
implicit-differentiation route is more efficient than the finite-difference
Jacobian obtained by repeated boundary-value-problem solves used in, e.g.,
\cite{kratchman2017guiding}, and is closer in spirit to the analytic
sensitivity boundary-value problem of \cite{wu2025closedloop}, though
formulated here around an energy-minimization rather than a shooting
solution of the rod equilibrium.

Because the magnet pose components of $\theta$ are realized by the robot
arm's own forward kinematics from its joint angles, $\theta_{\text{pose}} =
FK_r(q)$, the full configuration-space Jacobian used by the planner and
controllers is obtained by one further chain-rule step,
\begin{equation}
J(z) = J_b(\theta(z))\,\operatorname{blkdiag}\!\big(J_r(q),\,1\big)
\ \in\ \mathbb{R}^{6\times 7},
\label{eq:full-jacobian}
\end{equation}
with $J_r(q)=\partial FK_r/\partial q\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times6}$ the robot
arm's own (well-conditioned) manipulator Jacobian and $z=[q;L]$ as in
\eqref{eq:config}.

$J(z)$ is severely ill-conditioned: only a low-dimensional subspace of the
7-dimensional configuration space produces significant tip motion, a
consequence of the beam's weak magnetic lateral steering authority. This
is consistent with related single-external-magnet systems reported in the
literature, e.g.\ the rank-5 (of 6) magnet-to-wrench Jacobian reported for
a robot-manipulated tip magnet in \cite{kratchman2017guiding}, and motivates
the explicit singular-direction handling built into both controllers below
(damped least squares, Sec.~\ref{sec:dls}; anisotropic directional damping,
Sec.~\ref{sec:mpc}) — an alternative to the fixed-threshold Jacobian floor
used for a similar purpose in \cite{wu2025closedloop}.

\subsection{Offline Reference Trajectory Generation}
\label{sec:planner}

A reference trajectory is generated offline, in two stages, and supplied
to both closed-loop controllers as a feedforward term.

\subsubsection{Geometric path}
A sequence of waypoint configurations $\chi_j=[q_1,\dots,q_6,L]_j$ is found
by nonlinear least-squares, one waypoint at a time, minimizing the
residual
\begin{equation}
r(\chi_j) =
\begin{bmatrix}
\big(p_{\mathrm{tip}}(\chi_j)-p_{\mathrm{des},j}\big)/\tau_p \\
\big(t_{\mathrm{tip}}(\chi_j)-t_{\mathrm{des},j}\big)/\tau_t \\
\sqrt{w_c}\,(\chi_j-\chi_{j-1})/\sigma_c \\
\sqrt{w_0}\,(\chi_j-\chi_0)/\sigma_0
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:planner-residual}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_p,\tau_t$ are position/tangent tolerances used as residual
scales, the third block penalizes deviation from the previous waypoint
(continuity/step regularization, weight $w_c$, scale $\sigma_c$) and the
fourth penalizes deviation from a nominal seed configuration $\chi_0$
(weight $w_0$, scale $\sigma_0$), keeping the redundant configuration close
to a well-behaved posture. \eqref{eq:planner-residual} is minimized with a
trust-region nonlinear least-squares solver using the analytic Jacobian
\eqref{eq:full-jacobian}; because the problem is non-convex and the
Jacobian is ill-conditioned (Sec.~\ref{sec:jacobian}), each waypoint is
solved from several randomly-perturbed initializations (multistart) and
the best-converged solution is retained.

\subsubsection{Time parameterization}
The geometric path is then converted into a time-parameterized trajectory
by a reachability-based time-optimal path-parameterization method
\cite{bobrow1985timeoptimal,shinmckay1985minimumtime,phampham2018reachability}:
writing $x=\dot s^2$ and $u=\ddot s$ for a path parameter $s$, a backward
pass computes the controllable velocity set and a forward pass computes
the maximum feasible speed profile subject to per-joint and insertion
velocity/acceleration bounds (scaled by a fixed safety factor below their
physical limits). The resulting timing law is smoothed with a $C^2$
natural cubic spline to remove knot-velocity discontinuities, and its
total duration is dilated to the nearest exact multiple of the control
sample period $\Delta t$ so that the reference trajectory aligns exactly
with the control loop described in Sec.~\ref{sec:online}. This produces,
for every reference sample $k$, a reference configuration $z^*_k$ and a
reference (feedforward) rate $v^*_k$.

\subsection{Closed-Loop Control}
\label{sec:control}

Both controllers described below track the reference trajectory
$\{z^*_k,v^*_k\}$ produced by the planner, operating on the configuration
$z=[q;L]$ and its rate $v=\dot z$, and both reuse $v^*_k$ as a feedforward
term rather than re-deriving the whole trajectory online (the paper's
experimental section reports the comparison of pure feedback against
feedforward for both controllers; outside the scope of this excerpt). At
control step $k$
let $z_k$ denote the measured configuration, $p_k$ the tip position
estimate (from vision), $p^*_{k+1}$ the reference tip position at the next
sample, and $e_k=p^*_{k+1}-p_k$ the tracking error.

\subsubsection{Damped Least-Squares Feedback Controller}
\label{sec:dls}
The baseline controller re-evaluates the Jacobian at the \emph{currently
measured} configuration at every control tick, $J_k=J(z_k)$, and inverts
it with Tikhonov (damped-least-squares) regularization,
\begin{equation}
J_k^{+} = J_k^{\top}\big(J_kJ_k^{\top}+\lambda^2 I\big)^{-1},
\label{eq:dls}
\end{equation}
with fixed damping $\lambda$. The task-space command is
$v_{\text{task}} = J_k^{+}\big(k_p e_k/\Delta t\big)$; the redundant
(nullspace) degrees of freedom are used both to regulate the configuration
towards a reference posture $z^*_k$ and to inject the feedforward rate,
projected so that it cannot perturb the tracked output,
\begin{equation}
v_k = J_k^{+}\Big(\frac{k_p e_k}{\Delta t}\Big)
+ \big(I-J_k^{+}J_k\big)\Big(\frac{k_n(z^*_k-z_k)}{\Delta t} + v^*_k\Big).
\label{eq:dls-full}
\end{equation}
The commanded rate is then passed through a three-stage constraint
sequence — a velocity limit, an acceleration limit relative to the
previously applied command, a configuration (joint/insertion) box limit,
and a final re-clip of the velocity — applied identically for the MPC
controller below, so that the two controllers are compared under matched
actuation limits. An optional anisotropic variant replaces the isotropic
damping $\lambda^2 I$ in \eqref{eq:dls} with a singular-value-dependent
penalty built from the SVD of $J_k$, selectively damping only its
near-null directions; this mirrors the directional-damping mechanism of
the MPC controller (Sec.~\ref{sec:mpc}). This anisotropic variant is
implemented and validated independently, but its gain was set to zero
(plain isotropic damping only) in the comparison reported in this paper;
see Table~\ref{tab:params}.

\subsubsection{Output-Tracking Model Predictive Controller}
\label{sec:mpc}
The MPC controller predicts the configuration trajectory over a horizon of
$N$ steps under a single-integrator model, $z_{k+i+1}=z_{k+i}+\Delta t\,
v_{k+i}$, condensed as
\begin{equation}
Z = E z_k + S V,\qquad V=[v_k;\dots;v_{k+N-1}],
\label{eq:condensed}
\end{equation}
with $S$ block-lower-triangular ($\Delta t\,I$ blocks) and $E$ the
constant stacking matrix; a first-difference operator $D$ is used to
penalize the rate of change of the command sequence. The predicted tip
error over the horizon is obtained by linearizing the output map about the
reference trajectory,
\begin{equation}
\Delta Y = \bar J\,S\,V + c,
\label{eq:output-prediction}
\end{equation}
where $\bar J = \operatorname{blkdiag}(J_{k},\dots,J_{k+N-1})$ stacks a
Jacobian for each horizon step, and $c$ folds in the nominal-to-desired
tip offset together with an online bias estimate. Two Jacobian-scheduling
variants are compared: an \emph{LTI} controller that freezes $\bar J$ at a
single sample (evaluated once, offline, at one reference configuration —
e.g.\ the first sample of the current plan segment) for the entire
horizon and run, and an \emph{LTV} controller that uses a full schedule of
per-sample Jacobians, evaluated once, offline, along the planned reference
trajectory. Both variants therefore condition the prediction model on the
\emph{planned} trajectory rather than on the live measured configuration —
in contrast to the DLS controller's per-tick re-evaluation
(Sec.~\ref{sec:dls}) — a distinction with observable consequences for
tracking performance discussed in the paper's experimental section.

The receding-horizon command is obtained by solving the convex quadratic
program
\begin{align}
\min_{V}\ & (Z-Z^*)^{\top}\bar Q (Z-Z^*)
+ V^{\top}\bar R V
+ (DV)^{\top}\bar R_d (DV) \notag\\
& + \Delta Y^{\top}\bar Q_p\,\Delta Y
+ V^{\top}\Lambda_{dd}V
+ (z_N-z^*_N)^{\top} P (z_N-z^*_N)
\label{eq:mpc-cost}\\
\text{s.t.}\ & v_{\min}\le v_{k+i}\le v_{\max}, \notag\\
& \lVert v_{k+i}-v_{k+i-1}\rVert \le a_{\max}\Delta t, \notag\\
& z_{\min}\le z_{k+i}\le z_{\max}, \quad i=0,\dots,N-1, \notag
\end{align}
where $\bar Q$ is a (small) configuration-space tracking weight, $\bar R$
and $\bar R_d$ penalize input effort and input rate, and $\bar Q_p$ is the
primary tip-tracking weight. $\bar Q$ is deliberately tuned small relative
to $\bar Q_p$: driving the configuration towards the planned trajectory is
redundant with both the feedforward term and the output-tracking cost
$\Delta Y^\top\bar Q_p\Delta Y$ (all three pull the same finite command
budget), and an over-weighted $\bar Q$ was found to measurably degrade tip
tracking; $\bar Q$ is kept only large enough that the terminal weight
$P$ (below) remains well defined, since the beam's output Jacobian is
rank-deficient in the 7-dimensional configuration space
(Sec.~\ref{sec:jacobian}). $\Lambda_{dd}$ is an optional directional
damping term, built at each horizon step from the local Jacobian's
singular value decomposition $J_{k+i}=U\Sigma V^{\top}$ as a penalty
concentrated on its near-null right-singular directions (with a fixed
floor on the penalty weight, needed to preserve the numerical conditioning
of the underlying ADMM-based QP solve); it is the MPC analogue of the
anisotropic DLS damping mentioned in Sec.~\ref{sec:dls}, and was likewise
disabled (gain set to zero) in the comparison reported in this paper after
being found not to improve net tracking error on this reference path (see
Table~\ref{tab:params}). $z_N$ is the predicted terminal configuration (last block of $Z$) and
$P$ is the solution of the discrete algebraic Riccati equation (DARE) for
the single-integrator prediction model with combined terminal weight
$\bar Q + J_N^{\top}\bar Q_p J_N$ (state-tracking and output-tracking
weights evaluated at the horizon's terminal Jacobian $J_N$), approximating
the infinite-horizon cost-to-go beyond the horizon; this is also why
$\bar Q$ cannot be driven to exactly zero even though it is tuned small
(Sec.~\ref{sec:jacobian}'s rank-deficient $J$ would otherwise leave the
terminal weight rank-deficient and the DARE ill-posed). The
QP \eqref{eq:mpc-cost} is solved once per control step with the
operator-splitting solver OSQP~\cite{osqp2020}; only the first control
$v_k$ is applied, in standard receding-horizon fashion, before the state
estimate and reference index are updated and the problem is re-solved at
the next control tick.

\subsection{Real-Time System Architecture}
\label{sec:online}

Both controllers are executed in a single-process, multi-threaded runtime
at a fixed control rate. Camera-based tip tracking, robot joint angles,
and advancer insertion length are fused into a single state estimate at
each tick; the active controller is wrapped behind a common
$\mathrm{solve}(\text{estimate},\Delta t)\mapsto v_k$ interface so that the
DLS and MPC controllers of Sec.~\ref{sec:control} are interchangeable
without changes to the surrounding system. A supervisory monitor aborts
the control loop (commanding a stop on the robot and advancer) if the
vision state becomes stale, any worker thread stops responding, the
robot's own safety system reports a protective stop, the commanded tip
position leaves the calibrated workspace, or a non-finite command is
ever produced by the controller.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:expsetup}

The robot arm is a Universal Robots UR10e (6-DoF), addressed over its RTDE
interface at the manufacturer's native 500\,Hz update rate; a dedicated
streaming thread re-issues the current \texttt{speedJ}/\texttt{speedL}
command to the arm at 125\,Hz between control ticks (with a 10\,Hz
watchdog-triggered stop if commands stop arriving), while the
outer perception-and-control loop of Sec.~\ref{sec:online} — vision,
state estimation, and the controller solve of Sec.~\ref{sec:control} —
runs at 10\,Hz ($\Delta t=0.1$\,s). The actuating magnet is a grade-N52
cylindrical NdFeB permanent magnet (100\,mm diameter $\times$ 100\,mm
length) rigidly mounted on the robot's end-effector, with a dipole moment
fitted to $\approx$900\,A$\cdot$m$^2$ under the beam/magnet calibration in
effect for the reported experiments. The beam is a 2.0\,mm-diameter
elastomer/magnetic-particle composite (80\,wt.\% particle loading,
particle density 7450\,kg/m$^3$), fed by a serial-commanded stepper
advancer (USB serial, 115200\,baud, $\approx$50\,Hz dispatch rate,
0.166\,mm per step). Tip tracking uses a single overhead camera, with a
frame-grab thread draining frames at up to $\approx$250\,Hz (the achieved
rate is capped by the camera driver) and the reported tip position is used
directly from vision, without additional Kalman filtering, in the
experiments reported in this paper.

All perception, beam-model sensitivity evaluation, and controller solves
(both the DLS closed-form solve and the MPC's QP) run on a single
workstation's CPU — no GPU acceleration is used — within the
single-process, multi-threaded runtime of Sec.~\ref{sec:online}. Table~\ref{tab:hardware}
summarizes the compute platform and timing parameters; Table~\ref{tab:params}
summarizes the model and controller parameters used in the reported
comparison.
